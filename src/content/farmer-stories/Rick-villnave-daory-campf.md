Dairy Climate Adaptation and Mitigation Fellow **Rick Villnave** focuses on using cover crops to improve his farm's soil health and productivity. Rick owns and operates Villnave Family Farm in Cortland County, NY — a 250-acre operation growing field and vegetable crops including oats, corn, hay, sweet corn, pumpkins, cabbage, and winter squash.

The farm has a unique history. Once part of a ski slope, Villnave Family Farm has crop fields that are very steep and highly erodible, making erosion the farm's most significant climate-related challenge.

## Joining Dairy CAMF

Before joining the fellowship, Rick was unsure how he would fit in as a member of the program. He ultimately decided to join because he wanted to learn more about the role of climate change in agriculture. The seminars offered by the program helped Rick discover ways to improve his soil health and learn about grant opportunities available to farmers.

## Fighting Erosion With Cover Crops and Drainage

Erosion has washed away soils and entire crop yields on Villnave Family Farm in the past, necessitating retilling to restore fields to their original level. To prevent this, Rick has begun planting perennial hay as a cover crop to slow the movement of rainwater. Cover crops have increased soil moisture and helped hold the soil together, directly combating the farm's erosion challenges.

Rainstorms have also caused challenges with spillover in his ponds. To reduce the risk of runoff, pollution, and washout, Rick installed a tile drainage system that safely discharges excess water.

## Moving Toward No-Till

Rick is also working toward no-till crop practices, though the expense of replacing machinery is daunting. He has found that not tilling his fields protects them against erosion during intense rainstorms — and no-till remains a key goal for the farm's future.

## Sharing Knowledge With Others

In recent years, Rick has become an active voice in climate-adaptive farming outreach. He has hosted multiple classes of Cornell University students on his farm to share his practices firsthand, and has traveled to the Cornell University campus to give a presentation on the climate damage his farm has experienced and the steps he has taken to adapt.

Today, Rick continues to focus on cover crops as a means of reducing risk. In the future, he plans to implement no-till practices more broadly to protect his farm from the increasing threats of weather variability.
