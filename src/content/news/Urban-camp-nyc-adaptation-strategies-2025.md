Like their rural counterparts, urban farmers are facing more drought, heat, heavy rain, floods, and high wind events. Adapting to these challenges is not one-size-fits-all. In New York City, farmers experience unique micro-climates across the five boroughs, each created by its geographic location and surrounding infrastructure. Some farms experience strong winds caused by daily temperature swings. Others deal with frequent flooding and saltwater intrusion caused by rising sea levels.

In response to these challenges, the USDA Northeast Climate Hub and Cornell University's Cooperative Extension delivered the Urban Climate Adaptation and Mitigation Program (Urban CAMP). Since January 2025, participating NYC farmers and agricultural advisors have been exchanging evidence-based information and adaptation strategies to increase their on-farm resilience.

## Meet Jesse Miller, Spring Creek Towers Farm

One fellow, Jesse Miller, recently shared his thoughts and experiences surrounding adaptation strategies for resilience. As the farm manager at Spring Creek Towers Farm in Brooklyn, NY, Jesse first attended Urban CAMP to connect with other farmers about adaptation.

Spring Creek Towers Farm was established in 2011 (and reopened in 2023) within a tall affordable-housing complex in a large open field that borders Jamaica Bay. The farm property sits on a peninsula bordered by wetlands, freeways, and a former landfill turned state park — creating a set of challenges unlike almost any other farm in the city.

> "Now I know all these other people from CAMP that I can keep connecting with, and I learned about programs that I didn't know existed… We really need to be combining our knowledge as producers, in order to stay productive in the city." — Jesse Miller

While Jesse has farmed in rural areas before, he says farming in the city is a whole different experience.

> "Here in the urban environment, you are constantly thinking about climate because it's so extreme here." — Jesse Miller

## Wind

Persistent winds whip through the site at Spring Creek Towers, causing damage to both crops and infrastructure. Jesse set up a trellis system to mitigate the effects of high winds — but learned the hard way that materials matter.

> "We [now] use rebar posts and the thickest plastic that U-Line sells. Last year we used jute twine and didn't get the thickest rebar posts you can find. And everything just snapped. It's crazy because this isn't Illinois." — Jesse Miller

This year, almost every plant in the garden will be trellised, including crops not typically trellised. The farm is also working with USDA's Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS) to install a shelterbelt — a line of shrubs planted along the border of the garden. This shrub wall will slow down wind at the corners of the farm where it's strongest, minimizing crop damage.

## Heat and Pests

Significant temperature swings have stunted crops at Spring Creek Towers as well. Low tunnels made with agri-bond fabric and metal conduit have helped Jesse keep crops alive through the shoulder seasons, slowing plants from getting too hot or experiencing frost damage — but heat waves remain a persistent challenge.

> "Temperatures get really high in the spring during the day in the city. I can't always open up the low tunnels every day… I've switched to keeping all the conduits in the beds and putting shade cloth on them to reduce the heat in the summer. I'm using shade cloth on the cilantro and parsley, but even with the shade cloth the parsley went to seed. It's been really frustrating." — Jesse Miller

Heat events are also fueling new pest pressures. The urban heat island effect makes urban growing environments more favorable to tropical and heat-loving pests.

> "Pepper maggots are really coming into the city. They burrow into the seeds and then emerge from them, turning the entire pepper black inside […] During Urban CAMP we talked about what flowers to plant and how to keep a continuous bloom throughout the year so that we can have positive insects in the garden." — Jesse Miller

![Spring Creek Towers Farm crops and trellis infrastructure, Brooklyn NY](/images/news/urban-camp-jesse-2.jpg)

Promoting beneficial insects allows producers to reduce pest pressure without relying on sprays or trap crops that take up limited space. Typical crop rotation is nearly impossible in a dense urban environment.

> "It's kind of impossible to do crop rotation in the city when you have everybody on their apartment balconies growing whatever they want." — Jesse Miller

Instead of being able to pause production to let pest populations die out, urban farmers have come to expect that once a pest appears, it tends to stay. Learning about diverse pest management options has become essential.

## Building City-Wide Resilience

As Urban CAMP progresses through 2025, the program will continue to support NYC's agricultural adaptation journey as growers deal with micro-climates, heat waves, new pest challenges, and limited arable space. Participants like Jesse play an instrumental role — experimenting with new practices, identifying solutions for their farms, and sharing their knowledge with the broader urban farming community. The hope is that Urban CAMP participants will create more resilient food systems both on their own farms and on the farms of their colleagues.
